The founding and the four markets of Ǹrì

How the ritual authority of Ǹrì travelled along the market week, and what the lineage accounts record about the towns that received it.

Group Igbo Clan Ụ̀mụ̀nrì Place Ǹrì, Anambra Period pre-1500 – 1911 Source Oral history Source Academic

Recorded with the Ǹrì elders' council · Edited by Adaeze Ị̀kẹ̀jìànị̀ · Published 12 March 2026 · Version 3, revised 2 September 2026 · 2,900 words

The market week is the oldest calendar in this record. Èke, Orìè, Àfọ̀ and Ǹkwọ́ are not only days: they are places, obligations and routes, and the lineage accounts gathered at Ǹrì describe the spread of ritual authority as something that moved along them. To read the founding of Ǹrì as a single event with a single date is to read it in a form it was never kept in.

What follows is assembled from three kinds of material, and the kind is marked wherever it matters: testimony recorded with the elders' council in 2025; the district records of the colonial administration, which are used for what they counted and not for what they concluded; and the published scholarship on the market week and on ritual office in the northern Igbo towns.

The market week as a map

Each of the four days carries a named market, and the markets sit at a distance from one another that a trader could walk between them. The council's account describes this plainly: a man leaves on Èke and is home by Ǹkwọ́. The route is the week, and the week is the territory.

The accounts agree that the authority of the ézè Ǹrì was recognised in towns a long way from Ǹrì itself, and that recognition travelled with people who had reason to travel — smiths, traders, those carrying ritual obligation.1 It did not travel as conquest, and the record does not describe it that way.

Ǹrì did not take the towns. The towns sent for Ǹrì, and what they sent for was the cleansing, not the rule.

Testimony of the Ǹrì elders' council, recorded 14 June 2025, Ǹrì. Translated from Igbo; the recording and transcript are held in the archive.

What the colonial file counted

The district records of the early twentieth century are useful for one thing: they counted. Market attendance, tolls, the number of compounds, the distances between settlements. Those figures can be read against the oral account and they largely corroborate it on the question of reach.

The same records are not used here for their characterisations. The administrative file was written to make a system of obligation legible to a government that intended to replace it, and its vocabulary — "pagan priest", "fetish", "native court area" — describes the writer rather than the town. Where a figure from that source is used, the figure is given and the framing is left behind.2

A note on dating

No date earlier than the nineteenth century in this entry should be read as fixed. The lineage accounts count in reigns and in generations, and the conversion of reigns to years is an estimate with a wide margin. Where this entry gives a span rather than a year, that is the reason.

The four markets and the towns that held them

The towns named in the council's account as holding one of the four markets, in the order the account gives them, are recorded in the archive as separate entries, each with its own sources. Several of those entries are currently thin: a name, a location and the one line of testimony that names them. They are published in that state deliberately, so that the gap is visible and a town that wishes to fill it can see exactly what is missing.

The smithing vocabulary preserved in the testimony — the terms for the bellows, the ore, the finished blade — is linked word by word to the dictionary, so that a reader can follow a term from the history into its entry and hear it spoken.

What is still missing

Three things. The recordings from the 1970s referred to by two members of the council have not been located, and may be held privately. The market tolls for the years after 1906 are incomplete in the records consulted. And the account of the eastern towns rests on a single testimony; a second, independent account is needed before it can be treated as corroborated.

This section is part of the entry, not an admission appended to it. An archive that does not say what it lacks cannot be checked.
